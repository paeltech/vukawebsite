## The problem: fake inputs, real losses

For a smallholder farmer, a counterfeit input can put an entire growing season at risk. Fake seed, fertiliser and crop-protection products threaten the money and work invested in the next harvest. Evidence from Tanzania and neighbouring Uganda shows why authenticity matters, although the scale varies by product, place and year.

A familiar package cannot answer the buyer’s question: is this input genuine? Farmers need a check before use. Businesses need to protect trust in their products. Regulators need traceability that reaches the person buying the input.

- **Tanzania, historical estimate (2013): 25–30%.** Estimated fake share of certified seed used. USAID estimate cited in the [World Bank’s Tanzania Agriculture Public Expenditure Review (2022), paragraph 120](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099940006282220953/pdf/P1770630615f6e0e0bd4f0286e88583775.pdf#page=57).
- **Uganda, government plan (2025): up to 30%.** Estimated counterfeit share of the agro-inputs market, reported in the [Government of Uganda’s Fourth National Development Plan, paragraph 187](https://agriculture.go.ug/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Fourth-National-Development-Plan-NDPIV.pdf#page=114).

These estimates cover different products and periods. They are not a current East Africa-wide prevalence rate. Quality also varies by market: [Michelson et al. (2021)](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/misperceived-quality-fertiliser-in-tanzania) found good nutrient quality in sampled Tanzanian fertiliser despite farmer concerns about adulteration. Verification needs to address the trust gap as well as counterfeit risk.

## The solution: product trust, within reach

Hakiki connects a code on the product with a verification service reached through ordinary SMS. The person checking a product needs no smartphone, app or internet connection. Smallholder farmers can verify agricultural inputs before use, helping them avoid counterfeit products.

QR access provides an additional route for app users. Both channels start with a label on the package, connecting the buyer’s check with product information businesses maintain and regulators need.

Built in Tanzania, Hakiki addresses a wider challenge in low-income countries: making traceability useful to people at the end of the supply chain.

## How it works

The buyer starts with the verification label. On the Ruzuku example, they can scan the QR code with the Hakiki app or send the product keyword and verification code by SMS to 15054.

The response tells the buyer whether the code is recognised. A successful check returns product details, including manufacturing and expiry dates. An invalid code returns a contact for further help, giving the buyer a next step before using the product.

The Ruzuku product sticker comes from our project to trace sunflower seeds distributed under Tanzania’s government subsidy programme. Product stickers are attached directly to the packaging; this actual sticker is shown on illustrative packaging. The separate SMS conversation contains historical example codes and product dates.

## Value for communities, businesses and regulators

For communities, Hakiki offers a practical check before use. SMS access gives smallholder farmers and other buyers a way to make more informed decisions without requiring a smartphone or internet connection.

For businesses, the service lets customers verify products against their records. It helps legitimate producers and suppliers demonstrate authenticity, protect brand trust and support product traceability.

For regulators, connecting checks with product records supports oversight. The information can help authorities identify issues and guide follow-up.

## Government adoption

Hakiki has now been adopted by the Government of Tanzania. Deployment relationships include TOSCI, ASA, TTCL and AATF, connecting agricultural, regulatory and telecommunications stakeholders.

Government adoption is a milestone in a product designed to make verification part of everyday decisions.

## Vuka’s role

Hakiki was built through Vuka’s Studio Origination model. Product development, regulatory navigation and the route to market were part of the same build.

Basic-phone access, local language, variable connectivity and agricultural supply-chain realities shaped the experience from the start. Hakiki’s value lies in connecting those realities: a check communities can access, product trust businesses can support and traceability regulators can work with.
